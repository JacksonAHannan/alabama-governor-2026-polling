Methodology and assumptions

Alabama Governor 2026 according to current polling. Model snapshot: October 24, 2026. This is a conditional, two-candidate geographic model. The website serves a fixed published snapshot and does not automatically collect new polls or election returns.

The statewide polling average is matched exactly. Demographic and regional crosstabs are soft evidence. Historical local voting supplies much of the map’s geographic structure. The estimates are not reported votes, district polls, or probabilities of winning.

A material unresolved mismatch

The central model puts Jones at 51.2–51.4% among modeled voters aged 18–29. The source poll implies 75.0–77.2%, depending on undecided allocation. The model therefore discounts that crosstab by roughly 24–26 percentage points. A sensitivity with less restrictive age adjustments reaches about 63%; it does not resolve the discrepancy. Age, turnout, population proxies, and historical assumptions remain consequential uncertainties.

Statewide poll aggregation

The duplicate Alabama Daily News link is counted once. We combine three unique surveys in proportion to their full sample sizes: Cygnal 500 (26.3% weight), Lowry 600 (31.6%), and Napolitan 800 (42.1%). The latter is a registered-voter survey; the first two are likely-voter surveys. There is no recency, quality, mode, house-effect, or registered-to-likely-voter adjustment.

Full response rows are normalized for rounding. The original Lowry release supplies its precise numbers and separate independent-candidate support. Other-candidate and explicitly nonvoting responses are excluded from the two-candidate scenarios. With pooled Jones support D, Tuberville support R, and undecided support U, proportional Jones support is D/(D+R); equal-allocation support is (D+U/2)/(D+R+U).

Historical local voting and the May primary

The starting geography is the 2024 Harris–Trump precinct log-odds plus Jones’s 2020 advantage over Biden in log-odds. This assumes his relative local appeal in the 2020 Senate election remains informative for the 2026 governor election. Historical shares use (D+0.5)/(D+R+1) smoothing; crossover is stabilized by n/(n+50), where n is the smaller 2020 contest total.

We compare actual May Jones-versus-Tuberville nominee votes with that historical anchor. A primary-vote-weighted regression removes the association with an intercept, Black population share, and residual-race population share. Education, age, and regional associations are retained. The residual is stabilized by n/(n+50), centered statewide, and transferred at 25% strength in log-odds. Quarter strength and the 50-vote stabilization constants are analyst assumptions. The race adjustment is descriptive and does not identify a primary-turnout correction.

Primary votes were cast in separate party electorates. The nominee comparison is not the total Democratic-versus-Republican primary comparison. Countywide May votes with unknown residential precincts are allocated using the same party’s election-day precinct distribution. No named county or district has a special coefficient or forced winner. The 2017 Jones race and 2022 governor race provide historical context rather than direct targets for this map.

Turnout and Census demographic proxies

Each precinct retains its 2024 Harris-plus-Trump geographic vote weight. The 2,235,028 statewide units are not a 2026 turnout prediction. Within precincts, resident population fractions stand in for the electorate; differential turnout by race, age, or education is not separately estimated.

Race uses 2019–2023 citizen voting-age population: non-Hispanic White alone, non-Hispanic Black alone, and residual Other. Education uses 2020–2024 ACS C15002H for non-Hispanic white adults aged 25+, including noncitizens. Its BA-or-higher fraction is applied to white citizen adults aged 18+. Age uses ACS B01001 resident adults. These definitions differ from likely-voter samples and are not individual voter records.

There are 20 latent cells per precinct: White noncollege, White college, Black, and Other, each crossed with ages 18–29, 30–44, 45–54, 55–64, and 65+. Within each precinct the age distribution is assumed independent of race and education. College means a bachelor’s degree or higher; some college and associate degrees fall into noncollege.

Block-group citizen populations are allocated to blocks by 2020 voting-age population. Education proportions are applied within tracts; age proportions are similarly allocated. Twenty-five education tracts and seven age tracts require county fallbacks. The model does not extrapolate a separate 2026 population distribution.

Education and historical Black support

Otherwise comparable white graduates receive 2.1 times the odds of supporting Jones as white nongraduates, using the retained 2024 Fox/NORC presidential education contrast. This is a historical prior, not an observed 2026 governor crosstab. It is an odds ratio, not a fixed percentage-point advantage. Its conditional value is uniform across precincts and ages; local vote levels can still vary. Other races are not separately divided by education.

Each precinct receives an initial adjustment so the 20 cells reproduce its historical geographic prior exactly. Thus the education seed does not add another geographic education bonus. Black support starts at 90%; a separate statewide historical-support penalty also pulls modeled Black support toward 90% with a five-point scale. The historical reference is the retained expert-report transcription of 2020 exit polling. The different 2017 Moore contest is context, not a 96% target. Other support starts at an assumed 50%, without an observed ballot crosstab.

The fitted Black-cell Jones shares are 90.36% proportional and 89.95% equal. These are latent estimates. A precinct’s aggregate result, including a heavily Black precinct, is not a direct observation of individual Black voting.

Soft calibration and approximate regions

Numeric race, age, and regional targets come from Cygnal alone; all three surveys contribute to the statewide average. A constrained statewide intercept fixes the overall share. Other adjustments balance historical geography and crosstab targets by minimizing squared residuals with the following analyst-selected scales.

Smaller scales impose stronger penalties for a given departure. They are not sampling standard errors or confidence intervals. Zero-centered coefficient penalties use 0.35 log-odds for Black and age effects, 0.50 for Other, and 0.25 for markets. Effects are additive: common age shifts across groups and common market shifts across cells. There are no separately fitted education-by-region or age-by-race interactions.

Historical broadcaster county lists approximate Birmingham, Huntsville, Montgomery, and Mobile markets because the poll’s exact 2026 membership was unavailable. Other areas, including Dothan, retain a separate unobserved category. The Huntsville proxy contains Colbert, DeKalb, Franklin, Jackson, Lauderdale, Lawrence, Limestone, Madison, Marshall, and Morgan counties; it is much larger than Huntsville city or Madison County.

Districts, precinct boundaries, and map display

The model covers 1,947 2024 reporting precincts, 67 counties, and the 105 House and 35 Senate districts in the 2021 adopted plans. Historical and primary votes are transferred using 2020 Census block voting-age population, conserving source totals while assuming population-proportional preferences within source precincts. Precincts split between legislative districts use the same population allocation. The district totals are estimates, not official district canvasses.

Drilling into a House or Senate district displays only the portion of each allocated precinct inside that district. Display shapes are intersected with the same district boundary shown on the map, so excess precinct geometry is clipped away. Voting-age population (VAP) is the population aged 18 or older. Each portion uses its actual 2020 block-VAP count from the existing precinct-to-district crosswalk, divided by the precinct’s total block VAP. Physical land area is not used as a vote-allocation weight.

Each candidate’s modeled precinct vote weight is multiplied by that VAP fraction. The allocated portions sum to the existing district totals in both scenarios. Because both candidates receive the same fraction, candidate percentages and margins retain the model’s assumption of uniform preferences within a precinct. These are 2024 turnout-weight units, not measured ballots or a prediction of 2026 turnout. The panel shows the portion’s VAP and allocated weights. Historical comparisons and demographic percentages remain explicitly labeled whole-precinct context; clipping does not create new observations about individual blocks.

Search, map selection and shared links preserve the selected district and clipped precinct portion. The standalone statewide precinct view continues to show whole precincts. Fort Rucker’s zero 2024 vote weight stays unavailable rather than becoming 0% support.

District counts indicate modeled governor-vote leads above 50%, not actual legislative election winners. The browser reads the existing results and performs no model fitting. Shared-arc display geometry is simplified at a 50-meter interval and rounded to six decimal degrees, with documented tiny validity repairs; vote estimates are unchanged. Seven ten-point winner-margin color bins match the static maps, with the darkest bin at 60 points or more. Colors do not display uncertainty.

Uncertainty and sensitivity

This is a deterministic snapshot conditional on the pooled statewide result. It does not simulate polling error, correlated local errors, demographic measurement uncertainty, changed turnout, campaign developments, or legislative candidates. The supplied surveys and historical data do not uniquely identify individual voting behavior.

One-at-a-time sensitivity checks vary education effects, Other support, market weighting, May transfer, the historical anchor, Black support and its penalty, and age penalties. They yield 35–39 Jones House and 10 Senate districts under proportional allocation, and 37–39 House and 10–11 Senate districts under equal allocation. These are assumption ranges rather than confidence intervals or exhaustive bounds. The large young-voter disagreement remains unresolved.
